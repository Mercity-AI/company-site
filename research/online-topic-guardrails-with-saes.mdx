We tested whether small probes built on sparse autoencoder (SAE) features can catch an LLM drifting off topic while it is still writing. Each probe labels every token with one of seven business topics, so its label can switch between one sentence and the next when the topic changes. We trained small causal probes on the internal activations of Gemma 3 1B and Gemma 3 4B. 

Four in five of our records cover two to four topics. They move between topics in ordinary prose, with no heading to mark the change, so a probe scores well only if its labels switch where the response does. We gave the probes three kinds of inputs: 

1. Two come from the Gemma Scope 2 SAEs: a fixed set of features, and a set chosen again for each token. 
2. The third is the raw hidden states, which checks whether the SAE is needed at all. The figure below gives the best held-out macro-F1 for each kind of input, at both model sizes; its Table tab has the input widths and detector sizes.

- **Raw hidden states do as well as SAE features.** Without the SAE, the best detector is within 0.6 points of the best SAE input at 1B (96.7 against 97.3) and ahead of it at 4B (97.5 against 97.1).
- **A fixed SAE feature set chosen without labels breaks down at 4B.** Keeping the 150 most active features per layer drops the best detector from 96.2 at 1B to 92.4 at 4B. Choosing the same number of features with the topic labels brings it back to 96.4.
- **Detectors trained only on teacher-written text carry over to the model's own writing.** With dynamic SAE features, 94 to 95 percent of the tokens Gemma generates are labelled with a topic the prompt asked for, at both model sizes and for all three detectors.

## Why do we need topical guardrails?

A topical guardrail keeps a deployed assistant inside its remit. A billing assistant should answer billing questions, and a clinical assistant should stay clinical. Most deployments enforce this with a second model that classifies text: the user's prompt before generation, the assistant's response after it, or both. Checking the prompt is not enough, because a well-scoped question can still get an answer that wanders. An assistant asked to compare two health plans can finish the comparison and keep going. It moves into the tax treatment of the premiums, then the employer's leave policy, and no single sentence looks out of place. A classifier that reads the prompt never sees this drift.

The classifier that checks the response sits outside the generating model, which limits it in three ways:

- **It does not see the generation as it happens.** The classifier reads the prompt or the finished response. A topic change in the middle of a response is caught only after it has been written and, on a streaming product, read.
- **It labels whole texts.** A response that covers three topics gets one label, or a set of labels with no positions. It cannot say where the response moved from one topic to the next.
- **It works from the words alone.** "Risk management" means one thing in a clinical note and another in a loan review. The generating model has already resolved that from context. A separate classifier has to resolve it again from the text.

That design treats the generating model as a black box, even though the model has to keep track of what it is writing about in order to write it. Every token passes through a residual stream that later layers read to decide what comes next, and topic is one of the things that stream encodes. Our starting idea was to read that stream directly. A small causal probe, one that uses only the current and earlier tokens, could label the topic of every token as the model produces it. It would see a change of topic as it happens and could say where in the response the change took place.

Probes on activations and per-token labelling during generation both already existed, for other targets:

- [Anthropic's Constitutional Classifiers++](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04603) use linear probes on the model's activations as a first screen for jailbreaks.
- [Goodfire and Rakuten](https://www.goodfire.com/research/rakuten-sae-probes-for-pii-detection) label personal data token by token with probes on SAE features.
- Goodfire also [catches reward hacking](https://www.goodfire.com/research/reward-hacking-activation-monitors) with probes on activations.
- [Qwen3Guard-Stream](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.14276) labels each token as it is generated, but it is a separate model and its labels are safety categories.

We had not seen the two combined for topic, in a probe that reads the generating model and whose label can switch partway through a response.

We first reached for sparse autoencoder features rather than the raw hidden state, because a hidden state mixes many concepts in every dimension: a probe trained on it is hard to inspect, and its input grows with the width of the model. SAE features tend to carry one meaning each, so a guardrail built on them could be read and audited, and a small fixed set of them would keep the probe the same size at any model size.

The Rakuten result pointed the same way. Their SAE probes held up better than raw-activation probes when trained on synthetic data and tested on real data, which was our situation exactly: our labels would come from synthetic text, and a deployed probe reads what the model writes for itself.

The case for SAEs was not settled, though. [Kantamneni et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16681) and [Google DeepMind's interpretability team](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/) found SAE probes no better than probes on raw activations, and often worse. Goodfire's own [practical guide to SAEs](https://www.goodfire.com/blog/a-practical-guide-to-saes) recommends a plain probe when you already know the concept you are looking for, and a topic label is such a concept. So we did not want to assume the SAE helped. We trained the same detectors on the raw hidden states alongside the SAE features, so that the comparison would settle it for this task.

This log measures how well such probes work, on two model sizes and three detector architectures, through four questions:

- **Whether the SAE is needed.** The same detectors are trained on SAE features and on the raw hidden states the SAE reads, with everything else fixed.
- **How to choose SAE features.** Three selection rules, one without labels and two with, at the same budget.
- **Whether the detectors carry over to the model's own writing.** Only text written to order has a known topic for every token, so the detectors learn from teacher-written text. They then run, unchanged, on responses Gemma generates for itself.
- **Where the detectors look and how they break.** Layer knockout, feature attribution, and four perturbations of the test records.

## A quick primer on sparse autoencoders

A language model's hidden state at one layer is a vector of a few thousand numbers: 1,152 for Gemma 3 1B and 2,560 for Gemma 3 4B. The model stores far more concepts than it has dimensions, so each dimension carries a blend of many unrelated things. This is called [superposition](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.10652), and it is why you cannot point at one dimension and say what it means.

An autoencoder is a network trained to reproduce its input. It has two parts: an encoder maps the input to a new representation in a latent space, and a decoder maps that representation back to the input space. A [sparse autoencoder](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2023/monosemantic-features) makes the representation much wider than the input and forces almost all of it to be zero on any given input. It is trained on a large sample of the model's hidden states to reconstruct each one while keeping as few features active as possible.

The SAE has room for many more features than the hidden state has dimensions, but only a handful may be on at once. So each feature tends to settle on one thing: a clinical term, a dollar amount, contract language. Each feature is a direction in the hidden state, with an encoder weight that detects it and a decoder weight that writes it back.

We use [Gemma Scope 2](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior), Google DeepMind's open set of SAEs trained on every layer of every Gemma 3 model, and specifically its residual-stream SAEs with 16,384 features.

## How we set it up

### The data

The corpus consists of 10,000 synthetic conversations generated with [Simula](/research/building-simula-our-synthetic-data-generation-library), our synthetic data library, which follows the [reasoning-driven generation method of Davidson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29791) Simula plans coverage before it generates anything. A taxonomy defines the space of records and weighted strategies sample combinations from it. A generator model writes each record, and a critic model accepts or rejects it against structural rules.

For this dataset the taxonomy is hand-authored so that the topic sequence is the one controlled variable. Each record's topics, and their order, are fixed by the sampled combination before anything is written. The scenario and wording are left to the generator. This keeps the seven topics balanced and makes every ordered combination of topics reachable. GPT-5.6 Luna wrote and critiqued the records.

Each record is one exchange: a short user prompt and an assistant response of roughly 600 words, covering one to four topics. A multi-topic record covers the first topic fully and then moves to the next in ordinary prose. It never returns to a finished topic and never announces the change with a heading. Both prompt and response are segmented with inline tags, same topics in the same order, and the tags become the per-token labels. The seven topics are Enterprise documents, General news & content, Customer service, Legal, Financial, HR & people operations, and Healthcare. ### The pipeline

The backbones are Gemma 1B and 4B from the [Gemma 3 family](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19786), frozen the entire time. For each token we take the residual stream at the last ten layers (16-25 for the 1B model and 24-33 for the 4B model). Each layer's state is encoded by its SAE, a feature stage reduces the result to the detector's input, and a small causal detector emits a topic label for that token. Only the detector is trained. The backbone runs in two modes, prefill and decode: the detectors are trained in the first and deployed in the second.

- **Prefill.** A conversation that already exists is passed through the backbone in a single forward pass, with the response teacher-forced. Every token's activations come out at once and every token has a known topic. All training happens in prefill.
- **Decode.** The backbone generates its own response one token at a time. This is the deployment setting. Because the detectors are causal, the activations they see for a given token are computed the same way in both settings, so a detector trained in prefill can be run during decode without modification.

### Feature stages

The feature stage turns the ten layers of SAE output into the detector's input. Static selection keeps the same small set of features for every token. The input stays 1,500 wide, the detector stays small, and every column is a named feature. The cost is that a token loses any important feature that was not chosen. Dynamic selection keeps each token's own strongest features, so nothing a token relies on is cut, at the cost of an input tens of thousands of columns wide. Raw skips the SAE and hands the detector the hidden states themselves, which tells us whether the SAE adds anything a learned projection of the hidden state could not.

**Static selection** chooses features once. For each layer we keep 150 of the 16,384 SAE features and discard the rest, so every token is described by the same 1,500 columns. Which 150 to keep is decided by a selection rule, fitted on the training split only and then frozen. We use three rules.

- **Summation** sums each feature's activation over all training tokens and keeps the 150 largest. It needs no labels. A feature that fires on every topic ranks as high as one that fires on exactly one. This is the rule used wherever the text says "static" without qualification.
- **F-statistic** scores each feature by the ANOVA F-statistic against the token's topic: how far the feature's mean activation differs between topics, relative to its spread within a topic. It keeps the top 150. It uses the labels, scores one feature at a time, and is computed exactly in a single pass.
- **Group lasso** fits a linear probe from all 16,384 features to the seven topics under a [group-lasso penalty](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2005.00532.x) that deletes a feature's seven class weights together. The penalty is tuned until about 150 features survive per layer, and the survivors are kept. It uses the labels and scores features jointly. Some layers end with fewer than 150 survivors, so the total is 1,438 features at 1B and 1,489 at 4B.

**Dynamic selection** chooses features again for every token. Each token keeps its own 150 strongest features per layer. Each kept value is written into a column that permanently belongs to that feature, so a column always means the same thing while the set of non-zero columns changes from token to token. The columns are the union of every feature any training token picked, capped at the 6,000 most frequently picked per layer. The result is an input about 54,000 columns wide with roughly 1,380 non-zero entries per token.

**Raw** skips the SAE. The ten residual states are concatenated and handed to the detector directly: 11,520 values per token at 1B and 25,600 at 4B. Nothing is selected and no column has a meaning that can be looked up.

### Training

Every detector is trained with the settings below.

| | [GRU](https://arxiv.org/abs/1406.1078) | [Transformer](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) | [ConvNeXt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.03545) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Body | 3 recurrent layers, one direction | 2 causal attention layers, 8 heads | 6 [causal convolution blocks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01271), kernel 7 |
| Width | 256 | 256, feed-forward 512 | 256 channels |
| Context per token | Every earlier token, through a running state | Every earlier token | The last 379 tokens |
| Input and output | Linear projection to width 256; linear head over the 7 topics | Same | Same |
| Learning rate | 3e-4 | 3e-4 | 1e-3 |

After each epoch the detector is scored on the validation split, and the checkpoint with the best validation macro-F1 is the one we test. Every number in this log is from the held-out test split of 1,500 records. The viewer below has the training loss, validation macro-F1, token accuracy and gradient norm of all 30 detectors. Most detectors are still improving when training stops: 17 of the 30 reach their best validation score in the final epoch, and 23 in the last two. The exceptions are the weakest configurations. The 4B summation ConvNeXt peaks at 93.0 validation macro-F1 in epoch 9 and ends at 80.0, and the 4B summation GRU peaks at 88.0 in epoch 11 and ends at 83.2. Checkpoint selection keeps the peak in both cases, and the test scores in this log come from those epochs.

## Experiments and results

We ran six experiments, each on both model sizes and all three detectors. All scores are percentages, and every number is from the held-out test split.

### Baselines

The first question is whether SAE features carry a per-token topic signal at all, and whether picking features once is enough or each token needs its own.

> **Hypothesis:** Both feature stages should carry the signal. A fixed set, though, drops every feature it did not choose, so if some tokens rely on those features, per-token selection should score higher.

We trained the detectors on static (summation) and dynamic features and scored them in prefill.

We score each detector two ways:

- **Macro-F1**, the token-level F1 for each topic, averaged equally over the seven topics.
- **Overlap**, the intersection-over-union of the predicted and true tokens for each topic, averaged over topics. For the same predictions overlap is always the lower of the two, because F1 counts each correctly labelled token twice and overlap counts it once. A topic with 90 correct tokens, 10 missed and 10 wrongly claimed has an F1 of 90 and an overlap of 82.

At 1B the two feature stages are close: static selection scores 94.3 to 96.2 macro-F1 and dynamic 94.0 to 97.3, so a fixed 1,500 of the 163,840 available features (16,384 per layer across ten layers) is already enough for 94 or better. At 4B they separate, as the hypothesis predicted. Every static detector scores lower than its 1B counterpart, while dynamic selection holds, with the ConvNeXt at 97.1 against 97.3 at 1B. The budget is the same 150 features per layer at both sizes, so either the wider 4B model needs more of them or summation is choosing the wrong ones. The feature-selection experiment below separates the two.

ConvNeXt is the strongest detector in every configuration and gains the most from dynamic features (+4.7 macro-F1 at 4B). The Transformer barely moves at 1B (94.3 static, 94.0 dynamic) and gains about two points at 4B. The GRU is the most sensitive to a tight input: it has the lowest score in the table (87.4, static 4B) and recovers five points with dynamic features.

#### Prompt-and-response versus response-only supervision

The prompt also carries a tagged span for each topic it asks about, so a detector can be supervised on response tokens only or on prompt and response together. Supervising both adds about 515,000 prompt tokens to the 8.2M response tokens in the loss and lets the detector label the user's turn. Prompt tokens are fewer and carry less context, so they could pull the response scores down.

> **Hypothesis:** If the topic signal in the response is strong enough, the extra prompt tokens should not hurt. Two-sided supervision should hold response scores roughly constant and add labels for the prompt at no cost.

We trained every baseline both ways and compared them on the same response tokens.

### Decode: running on Gemma's own writing

Every prefill score comes from teacher-forced text that GPT-5.6 Luna wrote. In deployment the detector reads Gemma's own writing, which has no labels and a different style.

> **Hypothesis:** Gemma's answers differ from the teacher's in length, structure and register. If the detectors learned the teacher's style as much as the topic, their scores should fall off hard on Gemma's writing.

We let Gemma generate on 1,000 test prompts, with the topic tags removed, and ran the activations through the prefill-trained detectors, unchanged. As a reference, the same detectors read the teacher's response to the same prompts.

Gemma's responses have no tags, so there is no per-token answer key to score against. We compare the detector's labels with the topics the prompt asked for instead. Take a prompt that asks about Financial and Legal, and a 1,000-token response the detector labels as 600 Financial, 350 Legal and 50 HR tokens.

- **Purity** is the percentage of response tokens labelled with a requested topic. In the example it is 95.
- **Coverage** is the percentage of requested topics that receive at least a tenth of the tokens. Both requested topics clear that bar, so coverage is 100.
- **Full coverage** is the percentage of responses in which every requested topic clears it. The example response would count toward it.
- **Set precision** is the share of the topics the detector uses anywhere in a response that were actually requested. It is 67 here, since two of the three topics it used were requested. We use it when comparing the detectors below.

Purity asks whether the labels stay faithful to the request: a guardrail that tags a quarter of an on-brief answer with an unrequested topic would raise constant false alarms. Coverage asks the opposite question, whether every requested topic shows up in the labels. Neither score separates the detector from the writer. A low purity can mean the detector mislabelled on-topic text or that Gemma wandered off-topic, and a low coverage can mean the detector missed a topic or that Gemma never wrote about it. The teacher's answers help separate the two, because they are known to cover every requested topic. If a detector reaches full coverage on the teacher's text but not on Gemma's, the missing topics are more likely missing from Gemma's answer than from the detector's reading.

These results agree with a line of recent work on SAEs for downstream tasks. [Kantamneni et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.16681) tested SAE probes against probes on raw activations under data scarcity, class imbalance, label noise and covariate shift, and found no setting where SAEs consistently won. [Google DeepMind's interpretability team](https://www.alignmentforum.org/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/) reported that SAE probes underperformed linear probes at detecting harmful intent out of distribution, and deprioritised SAE research as a result. What the SAE does buy is size and legibility. A static detector is 1.4M to 3.6M parameters at either backbone, because its input is always 1,500 wide (Figure 10). A raw detector's input is ten times the hidden width, so it grows with the backbone. At inference the static SAE stage needs only the selected encoder rows, 1.73M parameters at 1B and 3.84M at 4B, against 378M and 839M for the ten SAEs loaded whole. And every static input column is a feature that can be looked up, which the raw vector's columns are not.

### Feature selection

Summation picks features by how much they fire, without looking at the labels, so a feature that fires on every topic ranks as high as one that marks a single topic.

> **Hypothesis:** If label-blind selection is what costs the static detectors accuracy, choosing features with the labels at the same budget should recover it. Group lasso, which picks features that work together, should also beat the F-statistic, which ranks each feature on its own.

We replaced summation with the F-statistic and with group lasso, at 150 features per layer, and repeated the baseline and decode experiments.

### Where the detectors look

Scores alone do not say which layers or features a detector relies on, and the selection rule could change that as much as it changes the score. A detector that leans on one layer is fragile: if that layer's signal shifts, the detector fails with it. We measured this with two methods, run on every trained detector:

- **Layer knockout.** We zero one layer's block of input features, re-score on the test split, and repeat for each of the ten layers. A layer's knockout drop is the overlap lost when it is zeroed. The largest drop shows the detector's worst case, and the sum of the ten drops shows how much it depends on individual layers overall.
- **Attribution.** We compute [gradient-times-input](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02685) for each response token's correct-topic logit, which measures how much each input feature pushed the detector toward the right topic. Within the layer with the largest knockout drop, we zero the ten highest-ranked features and, separately, the ten lowest-ranked.

> **Hypothesis:** The selection rule should change what a detector depends on, not only how well it scores. And if gradient attribution is trustworthy, zeroing its top-ranked features should hurt far more than zeroing its bottom-ranked ones.

We also check whether the two methods agree across layers, by the rank correlation between each layer's share of the attribution and its knockout drop.

### Four ways to break it

The test records are as clean as the training records, which real responses often are not. Topics come in the order the prompt asked for, every requested topic is answered, and nothing off-topic is mixed in. We edited the test records in four ways and re-scored the trained detectors on each edited record against its own clean original, running both versions through Gemma separately. Each edit takes away one thing a detector might lean on besides the text in front of it: the absence of off-topic text, the agreement between prompt and response, or the order of the topics.

- **Insertion.** An editor model, GPT-5.6 Terra, splices off-topic asides of a few sentences each into the seams between topics. Each aside is either a light joke (a pun, wordplay or an absurd fun fact) or plain small talk about the weather, food, travel or animals, and none touches any of the seven topics. The asides are excluded from scoring.
- **Removal.** One requested topic is deleted from the response and left in the prompt.
- **Reordering.** The response's topic passages are shuffled so that none sits where the prompt's order would put it.
- **Substitution.** One topic's response passage is replaced with a genuine passage on the same topic from a different test record. Topic and position are unchanged, so this is the control.

> **Hypothesis:** If the detectors read the topic from the text in front of them, they should lose little under any of the four edits, and nothing under substitution.

#### Reordering

The Transformer loses up to 5.0 macro-F1 and 8.2 overlap points when the response's topics come in a different order from the prompt's. ConvNeXt loses about one point. The Transformer can attend back to the prompt, and the training data always answers topics in the order they were asked. ConvNeXt reads a local window and has no access to that regularity. Under the static supervised rules the split is the same: the Transformer loses three to four points and ConvNeXt one. By topic, Enterprise documents and HR are again the most affected, and Healthcare and General news the least.

## Conclusion

A small causal detector can label the topic of every token an LLM writes by reading its residual stream. It reaches 96 to 97 macro-F1 on seven business topics, and it carries over to text the model generates for itself. The sparse autoencoder is not what makes this possible, since raw hidden states do as well or better. What a static SAE feature set offers is the same accuracy from a detector up to ten times smaller, with inputs that have names, on the condition that the features are chosen using the labels. The rules that do this are a one-pass statistic and a penalised linear probe.

Several directions follow from these results: (1) training the detectors on the backbone's own generations instead of teacher-written text, since the decode gap may largely be a difference in writing style; (2) adding a "none" class or a calibrated abstention threshold, so filler and off-topic text can be rejected instead of assigned; (3) using the detected features to [steer or stop generation](https://www.goodfire.com/research/understanding-and-steering-llama-3), not only to observe it; (4) selecting features jointly with the detector, or for robustness to a second writer; (5) larger and customer-defined topic sets; (6) other model families, larger models and wider SAE dictionaries.
